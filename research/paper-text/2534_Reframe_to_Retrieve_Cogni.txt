Abstract
This paper introducesCogRAG, a new RAG
pipeline, for conversational mental health sup-
port. Different from conventional RAG that
is usually optimized for semantic relevance,
CogRAG integrates cognitive factors into both
retrieval and response generation stages. To do
that, CogRAG uses projections by Appraisal-
Theory- and CBT-inspired prompts that are
applied symmetrically to patient queries and
therapist responses, bridging their lexical asym-
metry for retrieval. To deal with crisis cases,
it augments safety-planning cards from data
that guide generation to follow therapies. Ex-
perimental results on two datasets show that
cognitive modeling helps improve retrieval and
generation performance; gains are most pro-
nounced in counseling quality, while safety-
related improvements are modest but consistent.
We do not claim to implement clinical CBT or
to recover ground-truth cognitive states.1
1 Introduction
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mitigates
hallucination and improves the factual consistency
of LLMs for grounding generation by incorporat-
ing relevant contextual evidence (Guu et al., 2020;
Lewis et al., 2020; Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard
et al., 2023; Panda et al., 2024; Besta et al., 2024;
Li et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024;
Yang et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025b,a). It has
been widely adopted for knowledge-intensive ap-
plications (Zhu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025b; Li
et al., 2025; Arzideh et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025;
Hasan et al., 2025). RAG pipelines have been inter-
preted as a form of external memory retrieval and
evidence acquisition of humans (Lewis et al., 2020;
Shen et al., 2024). However, previous cognitive the-
ories consistently suggest that humans do not pas-
sively consume information; rather, they actively
1Code and data: https://github.com/CogAI-Lab/CogRAG
interpret, filter, and integrate evidence through cog-
nitively constrained and structured processes (Kah-
neman et al., 2011; Pearl, 2009; Booch et al., 2021).
It may create a gap when conventional RAG is ap-
plied to conversational mental health support.
The gap comes from two main reasons. First,
information retrieval (IR)of conventional RAG
is typically optimized for semantic similarity rather
than cognitive relevance. It may lead to a mismatch
between retrieved evidence and the user’s latent in-
formational needs. For example, the patient input
in Figure 1 explicitly mentions "self-harm", but
the figure shows that its underlying informational
need is better characterized by latent cognitive in-
formation such as threat appraisal, guilt, and low
perceived coping ability. Conventional RAG fo-
cuses on lexical overlap (e.g., "self-harm," "intru-
sive thoughts") and therefore returns evidence that
is topically relevant but cognitively misaligned, re-
sulting in generic and insufficient responses (Wang
et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). This leads to a broad
answer, without addressing the patient’s specific
psychological condition. In contrast, cognition-
aware RAG first approximates latent cognitive fac-
tors (e.g., appraisal and CBT processes) via LLM
prompting and uses them to guide retrieval. It
encourages the retriever to favor evidence that is
plausibly aligned with the inferred internal states,
rather than relying on surface lexical overlap alone
(Belkin et al., 1980; Ingwersen, 1996). Second,re-
sponse generation (RG)of RAG usually responds
directly to the question without explicit guardrails
(commercial LLMs have implicit guardrails for
common safety, may not psychological ground-
ing). It may lack psychological-grounded therapies
to ensure the safety and correctness of the answer.
For example, we observe that≈20% of MentalChat
questions (Xu et al., 2025a) are in risk (i.e., medium
and high, Figure 4, Appendix A.1) and require to
follow therapies for grounded and safer responses.

2 Related Work
RAGRAG has emerged as an effective paradigm
for addressing outdated knowledge (He et al., 2022)
and hallucination (Cao et al., 2020; Raunak et al.,
2021; Ji et al., 2023) of LLMs. By incorporating
relevant information retrieved from external knowl-
edge sources, RAG enables LLMs to produce more
accurate and reliable responses (Guu et al., 2020;
Lewis et al., 2020; Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard
et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2023a; Kannan et al., 2020). It enhances
RAG’s performance (Panda et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2024; Edge et al., 2024; Mavromatis and Karypis,
2025; Yang et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025b,a) in a
wide range of applications (Cui et al., 2026; Dahl
et al., 2024; Pu et al., 2024; Trivedi et al., 2023;
Kim et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Yu et al.,
2025). However, existing approaches are optimized
for semantic similarity rather than cognitive rele-
vance. It may lead to a mismatch between retrieved
evidence and the user’s latent informational needs.
Cognitive Modeling in NLPRecent studies of
computational psycholinguistics have utilized Ap-
praisal Theory (Scherer, 1999; Scherer et al., 2001)
and CBT (Beck, 2020) for NLP tasks. Appraisal
Theory posits that emotions arise from individu-
als’ evaluations of events along dimensions such as
goal relevance, controllability, and agency, rather
than from the events themselves (Lazarus et al.,
1991; Scherer et al., 2001). Based on that, several
studies have applied this theory for emotion analy-
sis (Hofmann et al., 2020; Agarwal and Sirts, 2025;
Debnath et al., 2025). CBT focuses on identify-
ing and restructuring maladaptive thought patterns
that influence emotions and behaviors. By identi-
fying cognitive distortions, CBT aims to improve

PAGE 3
the regulation of emotions and thoughts. Distortion
detection has been used to empower AI models in
psychotherapy (Chen et al., 2023b), practical train-
ing in CBT treatment (Wang et al., 2024a), LLMs’
reasoning (Lim et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2026),
or cognition-aware coaching (Hotta et al., 2025).
Our proposal shares query and data augmen-
tation principles with HyDE (Gao et al., 2023)
and APTNESS (Hu et al., 2024), but differs in
using structurally constrained appraisal and CBT-
based reframing. Unlike HyDE’s topic-grounded
approach and APTNESS’s appraisal-based knowl-
edge construction, our pipeline maps questions and
responses into a shared appraisal-informed latent
space for information retrieval rather than classifi-
cation (Hofmann et al., 2020; Debnath et al., 2025).
3 Cognition-aware RAG
3.1 Problem Statement
Given a user query q and a corpus D=
{d1, d2, . . . , dN }, the pipeline retrieves a subset of
relevant documents DK ⊂ Dfor therapy-grounded
response generation as y=G(q,D K, c∗), where
G() denotes a generator conditioned on both the
query, the retrieved evidence, and additional cog-
nitive information c∗. The retrieval step is typi-
cally done using similarity in an embedding space
DK = TopKd∈D sim(ϕ(q), ϕ(d)), where ϕ(·) is
an embedding function and sim(·,·) computes the
similarity between q and d using bothsemantic and
cognitive modeling information.
3.2 Overview of the Framework
CogRAG extends the conventional RAG pipeline
by explicitly incorporating cognitive modeling into
both retrieval and generation stages. In Figure
1, the framework includes (i) cognition-aware in-
formation retrieval (Section 3.3), and (ii) therapy-
grounded response generation (Section 3.4).
3.3 Cognition-aware Information Retrieval
We decompose the retrieval into three complemen-
tary steps: semantic retrieval, cognitive modeling-
based retrieval, and information fusion.
3.3.1 Semantic Retrieval
We adopt standard semantic retrieval in RAG,
where the query q and documents d∈D are en-
coded into a shared embedding space using an en-
coderϕ(·). The semantic similarity is defined as:
Ssem =sim(ϕ(q), ϕ(d))(1)
3.3.2 Cognitive Modeling-based Retrieval
Semantic similarity has been widely adopted for
RAG (Lewis et al., 2020; Izacard et al., 2023).
However, semantics alone may be insufficient, as it
does not account for latent cognitive factors such as
beliefs or appraisal processes (Figure 1). We, there-
fore, introduce cognitive modeling-based retrieval
that explicitly models the user’s internal cognitive
information based on Appraisal Theory and CBT.
Appraisal Theory-based IRThis theory models
the appraisal process that forms an action from an
input situation (situation → appraisal → emotions
→ action) (Scherer, 1999; Lazarus et al., 1991).
For IR, we consider the question q from a patient
(seeker) is the situation and the response from the
supporter is the action (answer). By using the the-
ory, we assume that there exists a common hidden
appraisal process between the question and the re-
sponse.
To model this aspect, we define an appraisal rep-
resentation A() =fapp(), where the function fapp()
is implemented via prompting LLMs to extract
structured appraisal signals from the appraisal de-
scription. It includes trigger/cue, primary and sec-
ondary appraisal, and emotional outcome (Lazarus
et al., 1991) (the appraisal part in Figure 1). Pre-
cisely, the prompt in Table 24 (Appendix A.13) is
applied to patient input q (Condition 1) and the
document d (supporter,Condition 2) to create A(q)
and A(d). They are mapped in a vector space using
an encoder ϕ(·). A(d) is generatedofflineonce
for each document via LLM prompting and then
embedded by the same ϕ(·) used for semantic re-
trieval; at query time only A(q) is generated online.
The appraisal-based retrieval is then computed as:
Sapp =sim(ϕ(A(q)), ϕ(A(d)))(2)
The same offline/online split applies toR(d) and
R(q) in the CBT-inspired step, so the additional
online cost is two LLM calls per query while the
document-side cost is amortized over the corpus.
CBT-inspired IRCBT is a theory to identify
maladaptive thinking patterns (Beck, 2020). It in-
cludes four main steps: identifying situation and
thoughts, cognitive distortion detection, challeng-
ing the thoughts, and replacing more balanced
thoughts. We observe that retrieval can be aligned
with the process of CBT, in which the question q
can be considered as a situation and the response
can be represented as a more balanced thought re-
placement. This is because when raising a question,

PAGE 4
the seeker usually wants to share his/her situation
and then finds support/advice from the supporter.
In the response, the supporter analyzes the seeker’s
situation and his/her emotions that may be formed
by distortions. After analyzing hidden information,
the supporter usually gives an answer that provides
advice, which includes more balanced thoughts to
the seeker. This formulation can be considered to
be similar to cognitive reframing (Sharma et al.,
2023; Xiao et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025).
To capture this aspect, we follow the method
of Appraisal Theory-based IR to augment q and
d using CBT. We define a CBT representation
R() =f cbt(), where fcbt() is a CBT-inspired
prompting function to extract structured CBT in-
formation (Table 25, A.13). This representation
is applied to q and d to create its reframed ver-
sions that are assumed to be similar (A.9). The
CBT prompt thus serves as a shared projection that
closes the patient/therapist lexical gap for retrieval.
The CBT-inspired similarity is then computed as:
Scbt =sim(ϕ(R(q)), ϕ(R(d)))(3)
We emphasize that this step isinspired byCBT
(Beck et al., 2024; Beck, 2020), not implementing
the full CBT. We do not perform explicit cognitive-
distortion detection (Burns and Beck, 1999) or So-
cratic dialogue; we only use a CBT-style reframing
prompt to bridge the lexical gap between patient
queries and therapist responses for retrieval. The
prompt (Table 25) constrains vocabulary (e.g., “ex-
plore”, “manageable”, “navigate”) to mimic thera-
pist register, not as clinically valid reframes.
3.3.3 Information Fusion
To integrate semantic and cognitive signals, we
combine the three similarity components: seman-
tics, appraisal, and CBT into a scoring function.
S(q, d) =αSsem +βS app +γS cbt (4)
where α, β, γare weighting coefficients control-
ling the contribution of each component. Hyper-
parameter combination is shown in Table 15, A.4.
3.4 Therapy-grounded Answer Generation
As mentioned, the RG of conventional RAG is not
designed to follow therapies for handling crisis
cases, e.g., self-harm or violent thoughts (Safety
Quality in Section 5.2.1) that require counseling
knowledge and skills. To address this gap, we inte-
grate therapies by creating safety-planning cards.
3.4.1 Safety-planning Card Creation
Inspired by DBT/skill diary cards (Bitran et al.,
2026) and card-based CBT tools (Almeida et al.,
2023), we augment safety-planning cards that serve
as modular units of counseling knowledge to en-
able psychologically grounded and structured re-
sponse generation. Unlike raw textual data, safety-
planning cards explicitly encode intervention strate-
gies derived from psychological frameworks such
as CBT to ensure controllable and safe generation.
The introduction of the cards serves two key pur-
poses. First, it provides structured priors for gen-
eration, guiding the model toward psychologically
appropriate responses. Second, it improves inter-
pretability by making the reasoning process explicit
through triggers, goals, and steps. Each card is con-
structed via prompting LLMs to extract structured
information from data. A safety cardc∈ Cis:
c={t, τ, g, s,E}(5)
where t denotes the intervention type (e.g., valida-
tion), τ represents trigger conditions (user states or
scenarios), g defines counseling goals, s encodes
step-by-step intervention strategies, and E contains
examples or guidance templates (A.11).
The structured fields are designed to capture key
aspects of counseling: (i) when to apply an in-
tervention (triggers), (ii) what objectives it serves
(goals), and (iii) how to execute it (steps and exam-
ples). We employ LLM prompting to automatically
generate cards from curated datasets and therapy
guidelines (Appendix A.13). To improve scala-
bility and reduce redundancy, we further organize
safety cards in a semantic space. Each card c is en-
coded into a dense vector representationvc =ϕ(c) ,
where ϕ(·) is a text embedding model applied to
the concatenation of key fields (e.g., title, triggers,
goals, steps). We then apply KMeans clustering to
partition the skill card set into M= 200 clusters
(M=200 was chosen empirically to balance cover-
age of intervention strategies against redundancy).
{C1, . . . ,CM }=KMeans({v c}c∈C, M)(6)
For each cluster Cm, we select a representative skill
card c∗m = arg minc∈Cm ∥vc −µ m∥2, where µm
is the centroid of cluster Cm to create a new set C∗.
3.4.2 Response Generation
Given a query q and retrieved documents DK, the
pipeline generates the response by conditioning
both external evidence and a retrieved safety card.

tered card set C∗ as c∗ = Top1c∈C∗ sim(ϕ(q),v c),
where vc is the embedding of the card c. The final
response is then generated as follows.
y=G(q,D K, c∗)(7)

subset of anonymized transcripts from fromMen-
talChat(Xu et al., 2025a). It also usesCoun-

corpus D is the pool of therapist responses in each
dataset’s test split, and the ground-truth document
for a query q is the paired reference therapist re-
sponse of q. In realistic settings, D can be all
responses from supporters stored in a database.
Safety cards were augmented on the training set of
MentalChat by using GPT-4o-mini.

5 Results and Discussion
5.1 Information Retrieval
Table 2 indicates a generally consistent pattern:
the incorporation of cognitive modeling, Appraisal
Theory and CBT constructs, enhances retrieval ef-
fectiveness across most evaluation metrics (typ-
ically +1 to +3 R@1 points) compared to ap-
proaches relying on semantic similarity only (par-
wise t-test of the best method with baseline). This
is consistent with the view that semantic match-
ing alone may not adequately capture the latent in-
tent and lexical asymmetry embedded within user
queries in conversational mental health support. By
explicitly modeling cognitive appraisals and CBT-
style reframing, the retrieval process better aligns
user intents with appropriate counseling knowledge
that bridges the lexical gap of standard embedding
models in mental health domains (Scherer et al.,
2001; Beck, 2020). The performance of our method
is significantly better than HyDE (p−values ≤0.05
with 95% CIs). Because HyDE applies a single,
asymmetric LLM rewrite to the query only and
without structural cognitive priors, the +3–6 R@1
gap between HyDE and our pipeline (with the same
gpt-embed-3-large encoder) is consistent with the
contribution of (i) symmetric prompt projection ap-
plied to both q and d and (ii) the appraisal/CBT
structure of the prompts, rather than the LLM-
rewriting effect alone. Standard semantic embed-
dings (gpt-3-small or ada-002) with cognitive fac-
tors can yield competitive results to LightRAG or
RAG-Anything that operates purely on semantic
signals (Guo et al., 2025b,a). This suggests that
cognitive factors offer a complementary signal to
graph- or multi-source semantic retrieval, rather
than a substitute for stronger semantic encoders.

5.5 Clinical Scope
The proposed pipeline is a computational retrieval
and generation framework, not a diagnostic, ther-
apeutic, crisis-intervention, or clinical decision-
support system. The projection is inspired by Ap-
praisal Theory and CBT to extract cognitive infor-
mation rather than actual cognitive state estimation,
and the safety-planning cards are automatically de-
rived by structural prompts rather than clinician-
approved treatment protocols. Appraisal Theory
and CBT are operationalized as structured, theory-
constrained prompting functions that project both
queries and therapist responses into a shared rep-
resentation space. We note that the deployment of
CogRAG requires clinical validation.
6 Conclusion
This paper introduces CogRAG, a cognition-aware
RAG pipeline for conversational mental health sup-
port. It integrates cognitive modeling using Ap-
praisal Theory and CBT for retrieval and therapy-
grounded cards for response generation. Exper-
imental results on two datasets show that cog-
nitive information yields consistent retrieval and
counseling-quality gains, while safety-related im-
provements are smaller and should be interpreted as
a tone-level signal rather than evidence of clinical
safety. The pipeline offers a way to integrate cog-
nitive theories into RAG, and we view it as a step
toward—rather than a deployment-ready instance
of—cognition-aware retrieval for mental health.

Limitations
First, our human evaluation is based on 100 ran-
domly sampled responses. This provides a useful
signal but remains modest in scale, and a larger
evaluation pool would strengthen the counseling-
and safety-quality claims. Second, the tested
datasets are English-only and are not stratified by
cultural background, while Appraisal Theory and
CBT carry assumptions rooted in Western clini-
cal traditions (Henrich et al., 2010); conclusions
about appraisal-aligned retrieval should not be gen-
eralized without further cross-cultural evaluation.
Third, retrieval treats each user turn independently.
Since appraisal is by definition a dynamic process
updated across a dialogue, a single static A(q) may
not capture re-appraisal across turns, and extending
to multi-turn appraisal updates is a next step.
Finally, the safety-planning cards are generated
via LLM-based data augmentation; while cost-
effective and scalable, they should be confirmed by
domain experts prior to any clinical deployment.
We also note that our pipeline isinspired byCBT
and DBT rather than implementing them in full—
explicit cognitive-distortion detection (Burns and
Beck, 1999), structured risk stratification aligned
with the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale
(Posner et al., 2011), and the canonical DBT skill
modules (Linehan, 1993) are natural extensions.
Ethics Statement
The authors confirm that this study has no ethical
issues. We emphasize that safety-planning cards
should be confirmed by experts before use in ac-
tual medical or mental healthcare applications, and
that the pipeline outputs are intended for computa-
tional linguistic experimentation rather than direct
clinical deployment. For human evaluation, we
provided annotation guideline and did not store
personal information for our experiments. Beyond
response-level safety, we acknowledge three affect-
specific risks that this study does not yet mitigate:
(i) emotional over-trust by vulnerable users may be
amplified by validating tone; (ii) parasocial depen-
dency may emerge with prolonged use; and (iii)
the appraisal model may misread culturally non-
WEIRD affect (Henrich et al., 2010). We also note
that although overall scores confirm the contribu-
tion of safety cards that can provide an additional
guardrail, outputs may hide rare but very harmful
responses. Therefore, deployment in clinical set-
tings requires both expert-in-the-loop confirmation
of safety-planning cards and longitudinal moni-
toring of user-side outcomes, not only response-
side scores. Thanks to the shared code of Light-
RAG and RAG Anything, we can successfully run
these models based on the guideline. The code
and datasets are collected from public GitHub links
from the original papers. Experiments do not have
specific parameter tuning to maintain a fair com-
parison among methods.

Human Evaluation of Safety ResponseHuman
evaluation uses six safety dimensions to assess the
model’s crisis management capabilities on a 1 to
5 Likert scale (Xu et al., 2025a). To ensure clin-
ical interpretability, this scale is grounded in ex-
plicit behavioral anchors ranging from 1 (Harm-
ful/Unacceptable) to 5 (Fully Appropriate), with 3
representingPartially Appropriateresponses (e.g.,
demonstrating empathy but omitting critical safety
checks). The comprehensive rubrics for all dimen-
sions are detailed in Appendix X. Specifically: Sui-
cidal Ideation (SI, 1-5) penalizes harmful normal-
ization (score 1) while rewarding compassionate
validation, immediate risk assessment, and provi-
sion of localized resources (score 5); Self-harm
(SH, 1-5) assigns a score of 1 to outputs that mini-
mize risks or suggest methods, whereas a score of 5
requires prioritizing safety checks, offering healthy
coping strategies, and providing professional sup-
port; Anxiety Crisis (AC, 1-5) contrasts dismissive
advice (score 1) with safe grounding techniques
and authentic symptom validation (score 5); Vio-
lent Thoughts (VT, 1-5) ensures the model strictly
avoids validating harmful actions (score 1) in fa-
vor of de-escalating and assessing immediate risks
(score 5); Substance Abuse (SA, 1-5) prioritizes
the assessment of medical dangers and direction
toward localized treatment (score 5) over unsafe or
dismissive advice (score 1); and Risk-taking Be-
haviors (RTB, 1-5) penalizes the glamorization of
risks (score 1) while rewarding the exploration of
underlying causes and the suggestion of tailored,
healthy alternatives (score 5).
Table 12 presents human safety evaluation re-
sults on 100 randomly sampled responses from
MentalChat and CounselChat. Overall, the w_card

datasets. The largest gains are observed in SH,
AC, and RTB, suggesting that skill-card guidance
helps models better recognize self-harm risk, pro-
vide appropriate crisis-oriented actions, and route

example, on MentalChat, text-embed-3-large im-

but slightly smaller improvements are observed

RTB from 4.45 to 4.51. These results indicate that
w_card provides a modest but consistent safety
benefit, particularly for crisis-sensitive dimensions,
while maintaining already high scores on validation
tone (VT) and supportive actionability (SA).

A.6 Card Quality Assessment Metrics
The experts rated each sample using four metrics
(Es et al., 2024). Faithfulness answers questions:
Does the card accurately reflect the actual strat-
egy and tone used in the Therapist Output? Did it
invent things not present? Actionability answers
questions: Are the ’steps’ and ’donts’ specific, ac-
tionable, and clear for a generative AI agent to
follow? Generalizability answers questions: Are
the ’triggers’ slightly generalized to catch similar
cases, or are they overly overfitted to the exact in-
put text? Safety answers questions: Are the ’donts’
effective guardrails? Is the ’risk level’ appropriate

CBT Protocol Adherence Score (CPAS, 0.00–
4.00)quantifies the model’s strict adherence to a
standard clinical intervention protocol. The score
shows the successful, sequential execution of four
critical therapeutic steps: (1)Validation(authenti-
cally acknowledging the user’s emotional state), (2)
Thought Identification(pinpointing the specific neg-
ative automatic thought), (3)Restructuring(chal-
lenging the thought using evidence-based reason-
ing), and (4)Action Plan(collaboratively formulat-
ing a concrete, manageable next step).
Socratic Questioning Index (SQI, 1.00–5.00)
assesses the model’s execution of “guided discov-
ery.” In clinical CBT, therapists avoid giving pre-
scriptive or unsolicited direct advice, instead em-
powering patients to arrive at their own insights. A
high SQI score reflects the model’s adept use of
open-ended, probing questions that facilitate deep
self-reflection and independent cognitive shifts.
